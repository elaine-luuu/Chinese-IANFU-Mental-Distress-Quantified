## Corpus

Mental distress among Chinese survivors is already documented qualitatively. Published accounts describe survivors' hatred or fear of men and difficulty with marriage and childbearing (Su, as reported by China Daily via SHNU); Tan Yuhua testifying with a deeply sad, stone-like face and without tears (Qiu); and Zhang Shuangbing's account of an interviewee sobbing for most of their first meeting, during which he wrote nothing down. Authors also note that rural chastity norms compounded the difficulty of disclosure and that few survivors in Yunnan would speak (China Daily). These accounts motivate symptom-based analysis; they are not themselves a representative or clinically assessed sample.

The following published works are candidate sources for the corpus:

| Source | Potential material | Check before use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Qiu, Su & Chen, *Chinese Comfort Women* (UBC Press, 2013 / Oxford University Press, 2014) | Twelve selected narratives from more than 100 cases documented by Su and Chen's Research Center; Part 2 contains survivor narratives and Part 3 covers postwar lives and redress. | This is an English translation. Locate and compare the Chinese source text, and record the edition and narrative/page boundaries used. |
| Zhang Shuangbing, 《炮楼里的女人》 (2011) | Accounts of survivors from Shanxi; Zhang visited more than 120 victims over roughly 30 years and helped file the first mainland complaint to Japan in 1992. | Published reports give different counts (123 victims versus 48 women in Yu County). Confirm the relevant sample size and scope from a physical copy before coding. |
| Su & Chen, 《日军"慰安妇"全史》 (2025) | A 2.16-million-character history reporting 300+ interviews across 20+ provinces and 358 mainland survivors located. | This is a general history, not a testimony collection. Extract and document only identifiable testimony passages; do not treat the whole book as a testimony corpus. |

Interview conditions are part of the data. Survivors could find it very difficult to speak to a stranger, particularly a man; Su began bringing Chen Lifei to interviews (Vassar Quarterly). Record interviewer identity and other available interview context when coding, and consider these conditions when interpreting silence, affect, and disclosure.

For context only, a 2004 *Journal of Korean Neuropsychiatric Association* study clinically assessed 26 Korean survivors: 30.8% met PTSD criteria, while all had experienced PTSD symptoms at some point, especially avoidance and recurrent distressing dreams. This is a benchmark from a different population and study design, not an estimate for Chinese survivors or a direct validation target for this corpus.

Before analysis, establish a source inventory with edition, original-language availability, passage boundaries, sample counts, interviewer/context metadata, and inclusion decisions. Keep selected testimony distinct from editorial narration and retrospective summaries.
